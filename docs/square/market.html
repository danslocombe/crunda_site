01  ·  Market Position

Crunda aims to sit within a lineage of short games built on one physically satisfying mechanic.
Its closest comparisons come from high-skill-ceiling movement games. For this analysis I have chosen to look at Clustertruck and Neon White on that basis, but also Pikuniku, which is a closer tone and price comparison.

All these titles landed mid-band or better commercially and every one of them had a publisher, a viral moment, or both. None succeeded on organic discovery alone.

Crunda's price at $12.99 sits deliberately in the Pikuniku / Clustertruck band, below the $19.99–24.99 short-form premium tier (Inside, Cocoon, Neon White) aiding conversion and setting expectations for a 3–5 hour game.

Within Collective's own catalogue, Crunda touches two reference points:

  • Circuit Superstars' replay depth - time attack, ghosts, leaderboards, simple inputs with a high ceiling.
  • PowerWash Simulator's inherently satisfying, inherently clippable physical process.

02  ·  Painful Admissions

Next Fest ticket was wasted

Valve allows one Next Fest per game, and the published guidance is not to enter below ~2,000 wishlists. In Zukowski's benchmarks, no game entering under 1,000 broke out of its tier.2 I entered at roughly 350 and came out at ~630. Why did I enter? Because I genuinely believed the game was almost done - a huge miscalculation, and a classic solo-developer scoping error. This mistake would not have happened with a publisher there to advise - I would have been told to wait on both counts. That estimate was also made before any act had gone from script to shipped build, the current timeline in the Core document is extrapolated from progress on the demo and Act 1.

The Steam data shows Crunda sits at 949 net wishlists after 21 months which is a weak number

No framing changes that. The claim is not that the number is good - it's that the number is uninformative: every exposure performed well - three at scale with measured conversion rates, two in person with jury selection and players competing at the stand (Section 6) - and the number simply records how rarely exposure happened.

03  ·  What the Steam Data Shows

The wishlist curve tracks promotional activity directly. Crunda's Steam page went live in November 2024.
Since then Crunda has reached an audience at scale three times - a video I made aimed at developers, a third party article and Steam Next Fest - and each produced a wishlist spike at a measured rate of 0.4–0.9%.
Two further in-person festival showings were validation events: audiences too small to read conversion from, but jury-selected entry and the strongest direct evidence the game works on contact (Section 6).

04  ·  Marketing Is the Most Important Thing

Third-party coverage converts roughly twice as well as self-promotion

Alpha Beta Gamer's coverage - an article with gameplay footage attached - had 22% of the visible reach of my own video (~11k footage views against ~50k) and produced 45% as many wishlists: ~0.9% per view against ~0.44%. The coverage was unsolicited, closed with "highly recommended", and linked the demo directly. (Note the article's readership beyond the embedded footage isn't visible to me, but the piece is footage-led, so views are a fair proxy.)

But the direction matches the industry pattern: someone else playing your game is an endorsement, you presenting it is an ad. Getting a game into creators' hands is something that I hope you can help with.

My video addressed developers, Brighton addressed industry. The only direct-to-player self-promotion ever attempted is a single TikTok clip (February 2025): posted from a cold account with no following, it reached ~2,000 viewers organically and drew comments, but the channel was never posted to again and the wishlists around it were flat.
Beyond that, Next Fest and Glasgow were the only true consumer exposure, and Next Fest happened at 350 wishlists with the same capsule art still on the page today. The storefront converts at 0.78% on cold browse against 8.7% on search and 13.6% on curator recommendation.3 When someone arrives with intent, the page converts; the first impression is the weak link - a capsule/key art commission is already a line in the core pitch deck's budget.

Who the audience is, concretely

A campaign has three natural channels, in order of evidence:

  • Creators adjacent to the comparisons - the Celeste / Neon White / Clustertruck coverage ecosystem, where one unsolicited pickup already converted at 2× self-promotion
  • The speedrun and time-attack community - ghosts, medals and global leaderboards are already in the demo, giving runners something to compete on from day one
  • Short-form clip channels - the wobble reads in three seconds of footage with no context, the same property a marketing partner monetised with PowerWash Simulator. (A single TikTok test in Feb 2025 reached ~2,000 organic views from a cold account - a hint the format travels, but too small to call evidence.)

Yet these people have not yet been directly approached.

05  ·  Beyond One Release

The Collective has a stated interest in projects that "grow beyond one release" or "evolve for an online audience". Leaderboards and ghost racing are built and in the current demo. The medal structure gives every level a competitive afterlife without live-service infrastructure (three inputs, deterministic levels, and seeded randomness).

Near-term, low-cost

Level packs are the natural DLC unit. The medal/ghost structure absorbs new levels without redesign, and the same editor that builds the main game builds the packs.

How much to invest on community tooling?

The in-game level editor exists in the codebase - it is the tool the game is built with. Shipping it to players is a nontrivial amount of work: hiding internals, and building UX for workflows that currently run through the in-game console. As a solo dev I want to prioritise effort to the critical path for release. So the plan for the editor is to ship as a post-launch update - which is also when it's worth the most, landing as a re-engagement beat into an existing community.

I am open to discussions on how much to invest here. At a minimum file-based level sharing is possible, a full online service with uploading, browsing, favouriting could be discussed and scoped together. Community levels for a speedrun game are a credible long-tail that keeps people engaged.

Projects after Crunda

Road Toads - Frogger turned into a competitive couch co-op experience. An existing prototype that has been played to death in private sessions, but not been taken to public events. This also has a Steam page and a demo available. When deciding a project to work on both Road Toads and Crunda were options but Crunda had a more proven track record.

06  ·  The Investment

As an investment, I believe Crunda has a limited downside and a high upside. One developer, a systems-built game, requiring guidance and a £50–60k budget - self-funded if need be - to reach a finished PC release.

  • Develop:Brighton 2026 Indie Showcase Finalist - 1 of 10 selected nationally, jury-selected, judged on innovation and originality.
  • At the stand, players understood the game immediately and needed no instruction and the competitive loop proved itself unprompted. On day two a physical high-score board went up and players got intensely competitive. When chasing a visible time, people replayed the same levels again and again; several played for 40 minutes straight - standing, in a crowded room, during a heatwave - and one attendee came back the next day to play again. However, medals alone didn't drive retries unless players were guided to them - visible, social competition did. That is what I can build into the shipping game with time tracking and ghosts from friends.
  • Ludum Dare 2021 - 1st place, "Fun" and 3rd overall of ~3,000 entries - the original prototype; the mechanic was the most-fun thing in the field on first contact.
  • Glasgow Independent Games Festival 2025 - exhibited.
  • Demo (Steam, Aug 2026 snapshot): 581 lifetime users; median playtime 9 minutes, 47% past the 10-minute mark. Most of those users arrived in Feb–Mar 2025, when the demo ran roughly 10–15 minutes at home - Alpha Beta Gamer's contemporaneous review calls it "about 10 minutes" - so the median cold-arriving player got through most of the demo they were given. However, the demo length wasn't version-tracked, so the numbers here are imprecise. The current 12-level build typically runs 10–20 minutes at a booth, with an observed range of about 7–30 minutes: newcomers spend their first 5–10 minutes learning the controls before playing in earnest, but experienced platformer players were able to pick it up right away. Downloads skew 45% North America / 31% Western Europe. Happy to share raw export data.
